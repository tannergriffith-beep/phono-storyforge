# 02 — Customer Personas

Phono has a clear split between the **buyer** (a parent) and the **end user of the instruction** (their child). The product must serve both, and their needs differ. There is also a third group — educators — who influence but do not buy.

> Note: The project's own `personas.md` is still partly a placeholder (several fields marked "fill in") and the customer feedback log is empty — the business is pre-launch and has not yet gathered real customer data. The personas below are synthesized from the strategy, research, and marketing materials. Treat unvalidated detail as a working hypothesis (see `08-open-questions.md`).

---

## Primary persona — The Home-Tutoring Parent ("The Waiting-Room Parent")

**Who they are**
- A parent (most often the mother), roughly 32–45 years old, of a child in grades K–8 who is dyslexic or visibly struggling to read.
- The child is often bright and verbal — strong reasoning and creativity — but reads slowly and with great effort, which makes the struggle confusing and worrying.
- Frequently has already been told by the school to "wait and see," or is stuck on an evaluation waitlist.
- Doing reading practice at home, often at night, with little confidence that they're doing it right.

**Emotional state**
- Scared, guilt-ridden, and afraid of making things worse. Worried the child is internalizing shame ("I'm the dumb kid").
- Motivated by love and urgency; aware that time matters and that waiting has a cost.

**Goals**
- Get structured, science-based help **now**, not in 6–12 months.
- Run effective sessions at home **without specialized training**.
- Know whether their child is "on track" and recognize the warning signs.
- Keep the child engaged and build confidence; prevent shame and avoidance.
- Sustain gains over the summer (avoid regression).

**Pain points**
- Cost and access barriers to specialists (waitlists, ~$2,000 evaluations, $75–$100/session tutoring).
- Not knowing if they're "doing it right" — methodology uncertainty.
- Time pressure — sessions must fit into family life; can't do 60–90 minute intensive programs.
- The child's emotional response — frustration, shame, avoidance, even physical complaints around reading time.
- Online misinformation versus trustworthy, evidence-based guidance.
- Isolation — no trained educator nearby to validate their instincts.

**Motivations**
- Trust their parental instinct that something is wrong.
- Act **before** a formal diagnosis rather than wait.
- Be a warm "coach," not a formal teacher.
- Understand the actual science (dyslexia is neurological, unrelated to intelligence, and responsive to the right help).
- Protect and surface the child's non-reading strengths.

**Common frustrations**
- School moves slowly; general classroom instruction doesn't address structured-literacy needs.
- Established programs feel overwhelming (many skill areas, long sessions, jargon).
- Guilt about not acting fast enough.

**Success criteria (what "it worked" looks like to them)**
- The child participates in 15–20 minute sessions without major resistance.
- The parent feels confident they're using evidence-based methods, not guessing.
- The child shows incremental progress and engages more willingly over weeks.
- The child's emotional relationship with reading improves (less shame, less avoidance).
- Summer skills are maintained (no regression).

## End-user persona — The Struggling Reader (the child, K–8)

**Who they are**
- A child in grades K–8 who struggles to match letters to sounds (the alphabetic principle).
- Often a fast, creative thinker with strong reasoning, but a slow, effortful reader.
- Experiences frustration, avoidance, and shame around reading.

**Needs**
- **Multisensory activities** (auditory + visual + tactile/kinesthetic) — engaging multiple pathways at once.
- **Short, focused sessions** with activity changes roughly every 5 minutes to sustain attention.
- **Immediate, unambiguous corrective feedback** — being shown the right answer in the moment rather than being left to guess.
- **No guessing from pictures or context** — direct decoding instead.
- **Frequent, genuine wins** and recognition of strengths to protect self-esteem.
- **Instructional-level text** — material where they miss no more than about 1 word in 10, so practice challenges without frustrating.
- **Story access** (e.g., audiobooks / audio-assisted reading) to keep vocabulary and comprehension growing even while decoding is still developing.

**Success criteria for the child**
- Willing participation; less avoidance.
- Visible, felt progress (e.g., reading a passage more smoothly on the third try).
- Growing confidence and a better emotional relationship with reading.

## Secondary persona — The Educator / Amplifier

**Who they are**
- Classroom teachers, reading interventionists, and speech-language pathologists aligned with the science of reading.

**Role in the business**
- **Credibility amplifiers**, not the core buyers. They validate the methodology, may use materials in their own work, and may share or recommend them to parents.

**Needs**
- Materials that are demonstrably evidence-aligned (IDA standards, Orton-Gillingham, structured literacy).
- Trustworthy, non-gimmicky claims they'd be comfortable endorsing.

## Jobs-to-be-done (what the customer "hires" Phono to do)

1. **Bridge the gap between awareness and specialist help** — give the parent something effective to do tonight instead of waiting months.
2. **Validate what they're seeing and teach self-assessment** — help them recognize the signs of dyslexia and decide whether to watch, look closer, or act now.
3. **Teach evidence-based practice without specialist training** — make the science doable in 15–20 minute sessions, with scripts, sequences, and feedback methods.
4. **Prevent summer reading regression** — short, distributed practice plus story immersion to hold gains over the break.
5. **Protect the child's self-esteem** — reframe dyslexia honestly (whole child, and needs specific help), celebrate small wins, and remove shame-inducing moments.

## The buyer ↔ user tension (important for design)

- The **parent** needs clarity, confidence, credibility, low time cost, and reassurance.
- The **child** needs short, multisensory, low-pressure, success-rich activities with immediate feedback.
- A single tool/session must satisfy both: easy for an untrained adult to deliver correctly, and engaging and non-shaming for the child.
